## Talking to Employer About Ethics

<!--
  id: talking-to-employer-about-ethics;
  type: activity;
  competency: 4.3.4;
-->

Explain and discuss when and where is the best time to bring up difficult topics to an employer.

Practice talking to their peers as if they were their boss.

Discuss the risks and nuance.

Connected to: 
- Prep reading showing unethical AI use case
- [Do No Harm Oath](https://www.wilmott.com/do-no-harm-in-the-age-of-the-black-box-a-hippocratic-oath-for-ai-practitioners/)

## Ethics Meeting

<!--
  id: ethics-meeting;
  competency: 6.3, 6.4, 6.5, 4.3.4;
-->

Student meets with professor in a mock meeting with their boss where they communicate (to the employer's dismay) that the AI use case they have been assigned is unethical.

This is meant to be difficult and give good feedback so that when this occurs with their actual employer, they are more than ready.

## Lunch with Professor

<!--
  id: lunch-with-professor;
  type: connection;
  competency: --;

Each group eats lunch with each other and the professor. While this doesn't meet any outcomes directly, it increases connection and belonging student to teacher and student to student, which makes all learning easier.

## Portfolio

<!--
  id: portfolio;
  type: assessment;
  competency: 4.2.1;
-->

Student compiles the work they have accomplished into a portfolio viewable by professors and industry professionals alike.

This portfolio includes GitHub links (and may be a GitHub repo itself). Don't prescribe too much if students already have somewhere they present their projects, but give unsure students clear options.

## Grade Letter

<!--
  id: grade-letter;
  type: assessment;
  competency: --;
-->

Student presents a grade they think they deserve based on their work in preparation for the Final Interview. Contains both structured facts and personal reflection. See `teaching_philosophy.md` for details.

## Final Interview

<!--
  id: final-interview;
  type: assessment, connection;
  competency: --;
-->

Student meets with the professor in a final, merciful, "judgement day" interview. The outcome is to determine the student's final grade, but there is also informal discussion about the class, what was valuable to them, and next steps. The student's grade letter helps by informing the teacher more objectively, but the professor's job is to be a just advocate.

## Out of Date Reading

<!--
  id: out-of-date-reading;
  competency: 5.3;
-->

Student given an out-of-date reading before class without being told. In their group discussion, they naively discuss what they learned. In the class discussion, the first question is "What are some signs that the articles you read are inaccurate?" The discussion becomes about that.

These can be sprinkled in more than once so they don't trust what they read too readily.

## Explain This

<!--
  id: explain-this;
  type: activity;
  competency: 4.1;
-->

Students are impromptu asked to explain a concept to their peer. 

For completeness, afterwards ask "Who feels like they've got it?" Depending on the room, either have someone explain it, or point out those who did not raise their hands and invite them to ask a question about the topic. 

Note how people are feeling about their ability, and don't leave anyone behind.

## Explain This - New Agent

<!--
  id: explain-this-new-agent;

  competency: 4.3.1;
-->

Class is given an agent to look at. They learn its core functionality. Then they are given a question at a time to explain to their peer, alternating who explains first.

## Hit the Board

## Pull

<!--
  id: pull;
  type: activity;
  competency: 3.4;
-->

Students are given an incredibly vague business objective. They are told to plan their agent, but come to a natural wall. They are forced to ask the professor questions to arduously pull all the context needed. They are invited to talk with their groups about what kinds of questions they need to ask. 

At a certain point, the instructor intervenes with any questions they missed and talk more about what they should be asking.

## Pull - AI

<!--
  id: pull-ai;
  type: practice, activity;
  competency: 3.4;
-->

For individual practice, the students are given a business objective, and have multiple AI personas they can talk to. They need to question them and pull out all the information they can. Results are compared in class.

## Make a Cost Assessment

<!--
  id: make-a-cost-assessment;
  type: activity;
  competency: 3.2.4;
-->

Students are given a real business objective with a known cost. In 5 minutes, they make a quick back of the napkin cost assessment and note what they need to explore to make a more accurate judgement call.

## Did it Work?

  competency: 3.2;

## Pass-offs

<!--
  id: pass-offs;
  type: assessment;
  competency: 1.1, 1.2;
-->

Each take-home pass-off directly corresponds to a competency. It comprises of a literal test case, and they need to build an agent that passes the case. Introduced in Unit 3 (when they have AI coding permission)

## Leaderboards

<!--
  id: leaderboards;
-->

Leaderboards show each student, their pass-off completion status, and speed of completion. This gamification not only provides extra incentive for completing pass-offs soon (due to peer pressure), but encourages fluency practice. Students can continue to practice similar exercises again and again to get faster.

## Coding Challenge

<!--
  id: coding-challenge;
-->

An in-class coding challenge definitively shows the student's speed and ability by giving a time limit to complete a few agent creation challenges.

## Outcome Self-Assessment Survey

<!--
  id: outcome-self-assessment-survey;
  competency: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6;
-->

Students are given a survey that is just a list of all the competencies, and they rate themselves for each.

| # | pseudo | stage       |
| - | ------ | ----------- |
| 0 | unable | --          |
| 1 | doable | acquisition |
| 2 | quick  | fluency     |
| 3 | expert | abstraction |

This can easily be a quiz in Canvas.

This is not a definitive test, but a good feedback mechanism for them to better understand where they are. Could be offered at the very start of the semester, the middle, and the end (or just before). This could also help them pick a final project that stretches them.

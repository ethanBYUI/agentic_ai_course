## Pass-offs

<!--
  id: pass-offs
  type: assessment
  competency: 1
-->

Each take-home pass-off directly corresponds to a competency. It comprises of a literal test case, and they need to build an agent that passes the case. Introduced in Unit 3 (when they have AI coding permission).

Optionally, we could do this in another framework than what they were taught in, forcing generalizability.

A few options for use:
- There are just a few cases that are bare minimum that they have to meet for an A
- There are many set cases, all pass/fail, with some that are required for an A (calling an API and instantiating an agent for example)
- There are many set cases, but students are not given a standard for how many they need to pass. 
    - Stress frequently how God grades - Does He have a set of pass/fail requirements? Very few, and they are mostly commitments. Outside that, He clarifies that those who do more good works are more blessed. His requirement is to mulitply talents, not how many to multiply.
- There are variable test cases. The test is revealed and a timer starts to implement it. They have as many attempts as they would like to improve their time, each attempt giving a different case. Students' times for each assessment show on the leaderboard.

## Leaderboards

<!--
  id: leaderboards
-->

Leaderboards show each student, their pass-off completion status, and speed of completion. This gamification not only provides extra incentive for completing pass-offs soon (due to peer pressure), but encourages fluency practice. Students can continue to practice similar exercises again and again to get faster.

## Pull

<!--
  id: pull
  type: activity
  competency: 3.4
-->

Students are given an incredibly vague business objective. They are told to plan their agent, but come to a natural wall. They are forced to ask the professor questions to arduously pull all the context needed. They are invited to talk with their groups about what kinds of questions they need to ask. 

At a certain point, the instructor intervenes with any questions they missed and talk more about what they should be asking.

## Written Planning Assessment

<!--
  id: written-planning-assessment
  type: assessment
  competency: 3.1, 3.2, 3.3.1, 3.3.2, 3.3.4, 3.4, 3.5, 3.6
-->

Generally, an outcome 3 summative written assessment.

Rather than a multiple choice or answer boxes, they are given a single task: Do their whiteboarding session for a single given business problem. It can be in any format that is useful to them as long as it is legible and meets the requirements.

This includes defined purpose, unknowns, quality dimensions needed, rubrics, good/bad examples, potential architectures with justification and notes about comparison, and a gut-check assessment on viability and cost.

## Project 3

<!--
  id: project-3
  type: assessment
  competency: 3, !3.3.4 
-->

Students are put in the shoes of an employee given a novel business problem to solve with an agent. They build the agent from the ground up.

Has all outcome 3 competencies written into documentation inside a repository for their agent. It is written so clear that an agent could one-shot the initial architecture.

From an implementation perspective, this needs to be simple enough that they have the resources to plan and code with a coding agent. It might be out of scope to test.

## Outcome Self-Assessment Survey

<!--
  id: outcome-self-assessment-survey
  type: reflection
  competency: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6
-->

Students are given a survey that is just a list of all the competencies, and they rate themselves for each.

| # | pseudo | stage       |
| - | ------ | ----------- |
| 0 | unable | --          |
| 1 | doable | acquisition |
| 2 | quick  | fluency     |
| 3 | expert | abstraction |

This can easily be a quiz in Canvas.

This is not a definitive test, but a good feedback mechanism for them to better understand where they are. Could be offered at the very start of the semester, the middle, and the end (or just before). This could also help them pick a final project that stretches them.

## Final Interview

<!--
  id: final-interview
  type: assessment, connection
  competency: 4.2.2
-->

Student meets with the professor in a final, merciful, "judgement day" interview. The outcome is to determine the student's final grade, but there is also informal discussion about the class, what was valuable to them, and next steps. The student's grade letter helps by informing the teacher more objectively, but the professor's job is to be a just advocate.
